1. The question: which infinite sums are irrational?

Every finite sum of fractions is a fraction, and an infinite sum can be one too: 12+14+18+⋯=1.\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\dots=1.21​+41​+81​+⋯=1. Now consider S=13+115+1255+165535+…,S=\frac13+\frac1{15}+\frac1{255}+\frac1{65535}+\dots,S=31​+151​+2551​+655351​+…, where the denominators are 22−1,24−1,28−1,216−1,…2^2-1,2^4-1,2^8-1,2^{16}-1,\dots22−1,24−1,28−1,216−1,… and the exponents double at each step. 2. Erdős’s question and the theorem

In a 1988 account, Erdős wrote [3, p. 105] (excerpt):

“let n1<n2<…n_1<n_2<\dotsn1​<n2​<…. Is it then true that ∑12nk−1\sum \frac{1}{2^{n_k}-1}∑2nk​−11​ is irrational, …”

Here the indices k=1,2,…k=1,2,\dotsk=1,2,… count the chosen exponents nkn_knk​. In these terms, we shall prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1. Let n1<n2<…n_1<n_2<\dotsn1​<n2​<… be an infinite sequence of positive integers. If ∑k=1∞1/nk<∞\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/n_k<\infty∑k=1∞​1/nk​<∞, then ∑k=1∞12nk−1\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^{n_k}-1}k=1∑∞​2nk​−11​ is irrational.

With A={n1,n2,… }A=\{n_1,n_2,\dots\}A={n1​,n2​,…} this series is SAS_ASA​, and the hypothesis says that ∑n∈A1/n<∞\sum_{n\in A}1/n<\infty∑n∈A​1/n<∞. Erdős’s question for arbitrary infinite AAA remains open [4].

Choosing every positive integer, on the other hand, puts the divergent harmonic series into the hypothesis. To see the divergence, group its terms as 111; 1/21/21/2; 1/3+1/41/3+1/41/3+1/4; 1/5+1/6+1/7+1/81/5+1/6+1/7+1/81/5+1/6+1/7+1/8; and so on. Each later block doubles the number of terms and halves their smallest value, so it too contributes at least 1/21/21/2, and these repeated contributions make the partial sums unbounded. The even exponents fail the hypothesis as well, since their reciprocal sum is half the harmonic series, and the theorem makes no assertion about either choice. Infinitude matters too: A={2,4}A=\{2,4\}A={2,4} has a finite reciprocal sum, but SA=13+115=25S_A=\tfrac13+\tfrac1{15}=\tfrac25SA​=31​+151​=52​ (Question 1).

Erdős proved the theorem in 1968 under the extra assumption that no two chosen exponents share a divisor greater than 111, and remarked that this assumption could be removed, without giving the details [2, p. 222]. We explain a proof that does without it.

The proof has three stages. First, rationality forces a fixed lower bound on certain positive remainders. Next, the special form of the denominators 2n−12^n-12n−1 lets us calculate those remainders. Finally, an average guarantees at least one choice whose total remainder lies below that lower bound. The doubling exponents show the construction at work before any averaging is needed, and the main proof ends in Section 7.2. Appendices A.1–A.4 justify the supporting facts about infinite sums and divisibility, and can be read as needed or after the main proof; the extension to other bases and the comparison with the formal proof are further reading.
